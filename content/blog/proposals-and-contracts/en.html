A new client emails on a Tuesday. You have a good call, they say "sounds great, let's go", and by Thursday you are already sketching. It is how most freelance projects end up bigger, slower and later to pay than anyone planned. This article walks through a short, repeatable way to get a real commitment before you start: a clear proposal, a contract with plain terms, and a deposit that arrives before the first hour of work.

Why starting on a handshake hurts

A handshake is not dishonest. Both sides usually mean well. The problem is that each of you leaves the call with a slightly different picture of the project, and nothing written down to compare them against.

  • Scope creep. "Could you also do the social templates?" is a reasonable question. Without a written scope, you have no neutral way to say "that's outside what we agreed, here's the price."
  • Endless revisions. If you never said how many rounds of changes are included, the honest answer is "as many as it takes," which is rarely what you priced.
  • Late payment. If payment terms were never agreed, the client's accounts team will apply their own. That can mean net 60, or "when the project is finished," which may be months away.
  • No easy exit. When a client goes quiet halfway through, you have nothing that says what happens to the work done so far, or what you are owed for it.

None of this needs a lawyer or a 20-page document. It needs two short pieces of paper, signed in the right order.

What a good proposal contains

A proposal answers one question for the client: "What exactly do I get, when, and for how much?" Keep it short enough that they actually read it. One or two pages is plenty for most projects.

  1. The problem in their words. Two or three sentences that show you listened. "Juniper is launching a second product line in March and the current logo doesn't stretch to it."
  2. Scope. What you will do, and just as important, what you won't. "Logo, colour palette, type system and a 12-page brand guide. Website design is not included."
  3. Deliverables. The actual files or outcomes they will receive. "Logo in SVG, PNG and PDF; brand guide as PDF; source files."
  4. Timeline. Start date, key milestones, delivery date, and what you need from them to hit it. "Feedback on concepts within five working days."
  5. Price. A clear total, for example €4,800, and how it will be billed. More on that below.
  6. Options, if useful. Two or three packages let the client choose instead of haggle. "Essentials €3,200 / Complete €4,800 / Complete + launch kit €6,400." Nobody has to negotiate.

Then ask for a clear yes. Not "let me know what you think," but "accept online when you're ready and I'll send the agreement."

Let the client accept online

A PDF attached to an email asks the client to do work: download it, print or annotate it, reply with "yes, approved." A link they open in the browser, with one button, gets answered faster and leaves a cleaner record.

When the client accepts, you want to keep three things: what they agreed to (the exact version), who agreed, and when. If you offered packages, you also want to know which one they picked, at what price. That record is what you point to later when "could you also..." comes up.

Turn the accepted proposal into a contract

The proposal says what you'll do. The contract says how you'll work together while you do it, and what happens if things change. For many small projects you can combine the two in one document. For larger ones, a separate contract keeps things tidy.

What follows is general guidance, not legal advice. Rules differ by country, and for large or unusual projects it is worth having a lawyer look over your standard contract once. Most freelance contracts cover these points:

  • Scope. Refer to the accepted proposal, and say how extra work is handled. "Changes outside the agreed scope are quoted separately and start only after written approval."
  • Revisions. How many rounds are included and what counts as a round. "Two rounds of consolidated feedback per deliverable. Further rounds are billed at €90 per hour."
  • Payment terms. Amounts, due dates and what happens if payment is late. "Invoices are due within 14 days (net 14). Work pauses if a payment is more than 14 days overdue."
  • Intellectual property. Who owns the final work and when ownership transfers. A common approach: rights transfer to the client on full payment, and you may show the work in your portfolio.
  • Cancellation. How either side can end the project, with how much notice, and what is owed. "The deposit is non-refundable. If the project is cancelled after concepts are delivered, 75% of the fee is due."
  • Client responsibilities. Content, access and feedback you need, and by when. Many delays start on the client's side, and this makes that visible.

Write it in plain language. A contract the client understands is a contract they are more likely to respect.

Set the deposit and billing schedule before work starts

The deposit is the moment the commitment becomes real, for both of you. A common setup on a €4,800 project is 50% on signature (€2,400) and 50% on delivery. Longer projects often split into three: 40% to start, 30% at a midpoint milestone, 30% on delivery.

Agree this schedule in the proposal or contract, not in a follow-up email. Then make the first invoice go out the day they sign, and don't start until it's paid. That last part is the hard one, and it's the one that protects you most. For the invoice itself, from numbering to payment details and follow-ups, see how to invoice clients.

A simple sequence: inquiry to kickoff

Here is the whole flow in five steps. Use it as a checklist for every new project.

  1. Inquiry. The client fills in a short form or emails you. Get the basics: what they need, the deadline, a rough budget.
  2. Proposal. Within two working days, send scope, deliverables, timeline, price and options, with an online accept button.
  3. Contract. Once accepted, send the agreement with revisions, payment terms, IP and cancellation. The client signs.
  4. Deposit. The first invoice goes out on signature. You wait for payment.
  5. Kickoff. Deposit received, project moves to "in progress", you book the kickoff call.
Rule of thumb: no signed agreement and no deposit, no work. Not even "a few quick ideas to get started."

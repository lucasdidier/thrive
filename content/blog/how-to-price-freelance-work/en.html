Most freelancers set their first price by looking at what others charge and going a little lower. Then they spend years wondering why they are busy and still short of money. A good price starts with your own numbers: what you need to earn, what it costs to run your business, and how many hours you can really bill. From there, the pricing model is a choice you make per client and per project.

Start with your minimum hourly rate

Your minimum rate is the floor, not the price you quote. Below it, you are working at a loss. You work it out in four steps.

  1. Income goal. What you want to take home in a year, after tax. Say €50,000.
  2. Taxes and contributions. Income tax and social contributions depend heavily on your country and status, so ask an accountant for your real figure. In this example we set aside 30% of profit. To keep €50,000, you need about €71,400 of profit (€50,000 ÷ 0.7).
  3. Business costs. Software, hardware, insurance, an accountant, a coworking desk, training. Say €6,000 a year. Revenue needed: €71,400 + €6,000 = €77,400.
  4. Realistic billable hours. This is where most people go wrong.

A year has 52 weeks. Take off 5 weeks of holiday and about a week of sick days and public holidays, and you have 46 working weeks. You might work 40 hours a week, but not all of it is billable. Sales calls, proposals, admin, invoicing and learning take a real share. For most freelancers, 50–65% billable is normal. At 60%, that is 24 billable hours a week, or 1,104 hours a year.

€77,400 ÷ 1,104 hours = €70 an hour. That is your minimum. Round up to €75 so a slow month or a late payment doesn't break the plan, and treat €90 or more as your target for good-fit work.

Choose a pricing model that fits the work

Your minimum rate stays the same. What changes is how you package it for the client.

Hourly

Good when the scope is unclear or keeps moving: maintenance, bug fixing, ongoing consulting, "help us figure this out" work. The downside: you earn less as you get faster, and clients can feel they are watching a meter.

Day rate

Good for workshops, on-site work, photography shoots and embedded work with a team. Clients like it because it is easy to budget. A simple way to set it: your hourly rate times the billable hours in a day, for example €75 × 7 = €525, rounded to €550. A booked day is a day you cannot sell to anyone else, so don't discount it much.

Fixed price

Good when the deliverables are clear: a brand identity, a website, a set of product photos. The client knows the total up front, and you earn more when you work efficiently. The risk is all yours if you underestimate, which is why the scope, number of revisions and what costs extra must be written down.

Packages

Fixed prices for predefined bundles: "Essentials", "Standard", "Complete". They work well when you do similar projects again and again. The client compares your options instead of comparing you to other freelancers.

Retainers

A monthly fee for a set amount of work or availability, for example 20 hours a month for €1,600. Good for long-term clients who need steady help. Agree what happens to unused hours (usually they don't roll over) and how extra hours are billed.

Estimate fixed-price projects from your own history

A fixed price is only as good as your estimate, and your best estimate comes from projects you have already done. If you track your time, you have this data.

Say your last five website projects took 48, 52, 55, 58 and 62 hours. The average is 55 hours. Your estimate for the next similar project:

  • 55 hours × €90 target rate = €4,950.
  • Add a 15% buffer for the unknowns (late content, extra calls): about €5,700.
  • Check it against your minimum: even at 70 hours, €5,700 is still above €75 an hour.

Quote €5,700, not €4,950. If you have no history yet, break the project into phases (discovery, design, build, revisions, launch), estimate each one, and add a larger buffer. Then start tracking, so the next quote is better. Our guide to time tracking for freelancers shows how to turn hours into pricing data.

Offer two or three options in your proposal

When you send one price, the client's question is "yes or no?" When you send three, the question becomes "which one?" That is a much better conversation to have.

For the website above, it could look like this:

  • Essentials, €3,900: five-page site on a proven layout, one round of revisions.
  • Standard, €5,700: up to ten pages, custom design, two rounds of revisions, basic SEO setup.
  • Complete, €7,400: everything in Standard, plus copy editing, a launch checklist and a month of support.

The top option does two jobs. Some clients will choose it, and it makes the middle option look reasonable next to it. That is anchoring, and it works without any pressure. Each option should be a real offer you are happy to deliver, with clear differences in scope. And make sure the cheapest option is still above your minimum rate.

Ask for a deposit

For fixed-price and package work, ask for part of the fee before you start. 50% is common for projects under a few thousand euros. For larger projects, 30–50% up front, then milestones. A €5,700 project might be €2,850 on acceptance, €1,425 when the design is approved and €1,425 at launch.

A deposit confirms the client is committed, covers you if the project stalls, and means you are never weeks of work out of pocket. Put it in the proposal, so it is agreed before the first invoice arrives.

Raise your rates for existing clients

New clients get your new prices from day one. Existing clients need a little more care, but they still need to move. Your costs go up every year, and so does your skill.

  • Give notice. 30 to 60 days is fair. Name the date the new rate starts.
  • Be clear, not apologetic. "From 1 February, my hourly rate will be €90." You don't need to justify it at length.
  • Raise in steps. 10–15% a year is easier to accept than 40% after three years of silence.
  • Honour what is signed. Agreed projects keep their price.

Check profitability after every project

Pricing is a loop, not a one-time decision. When a project ends, take ten minutes:

  • Divide the fee by the hours you actually worked. That is your effective hourly rate.
  • Compare it with your minimum (€75) and your target (€90).
  • Note where the extra hours went: revisions, calls, unclear briefs, waiting for content.
  • Adjust the next quote, the package contents, or the revision limit.

A €5,700 project that took 60 hours paid €95 an hour. Good. One that took 85 hours paid €67, below your floor. That is a sign to raise the price or tighten the scope, not to work faster next time.

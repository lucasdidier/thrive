The contract is signed and the deposit is on its way. This is the moment most freelancers relax, and it is the moment clients pay the closest attention. The first two weeks set the tone for the whole project: whether feedback arrives on time, whether you chase files for days, whether the client trusts your process. A simple, repeatable onboarding routine takes care of all of that.

Why onboarding deserves a process

Without a routine, onboarding happens in fragments. A quick "excited to start!" email. A request for the logo files two days later. Then the brand guidelines, then a login, then a question about who approves the work. Each request is reasonable. Together they push your real start date back by a week.

On a €4,800 brand project with a six-week timeline, that week is expensive. A good onboarding process does three things up front:

  • Collects everything you need in one go, so you can start on day one.
  • Sets expectations about communication, feedback and timing before anyone has a reason to be frustrated.
  • Gives the client one place to look, so documents and invoices never get lost.

Start onboarding when the deal is actually done

Pick a clear trigger and stick to it. For most freelancers, onboarding starts when the contract is signed and the deposit invoice has gone out. Some wait until the deposit is paid; that is a good rule with new clients you do not know yet. What matters is that you never start work on a handshake. Step 1: Send a welcome message the same day

Send it within a few hours of the signature, while the client is still excited. It should be short and do four jobs: thank them, confirm what happens next, ask for the one thing you need right away, and introduce where everything will live.

Subject: Welcome aboard: next steps for your brand identity

Hi Sam,

Thanks for signing. We are excited to get started.

Here is what happens next. First, please fill in our short onboarding form (about 15 minutes) so we have your goals, brand assets and the right contacts: [link]. Second, book a 45-minute kickoff call for next week. Everything for this project, including the contract, invoices and forms, lives on one private page: [link].

The deposit invoice is on that page too. Work starts once it is settled.

Best,
Alex

One form, one call, one page. No wall of attachments.

Step 2: Collect what you need with a form

Asking for information by email gets you half-answers spread across replies. A form gets you complete, structured answers in one submission. Ask for everything you need to start, and nothing you do not:

  • Contacts: the main contact, and who reviews and approves the work. These are often different people.
  • Goals: what a successful project looks like, in the client's words.
  • Assets: logos, brand guidelines, copy, photos, existing documents.
  • Access: which accounts you will need (website, hosting, social, analytics). Ask which access is needed and how they prefer to share it, but never collect passwords in a form. Ask them to invite you as a user or use a password manager's sharing feature instead.
  • Preferences: how they like to communicate, and any dates when they are unavailable.

Keep it under ten questions, or the client will put it off.

Step 3: Run a focused kickoff call

Book the call after the form comes back, not before. That way you spend the call discussing answers instead of collecting them. Forty-five minutes is enough for most projects. A simple agenda:

  1. Goals (10 min): walk through their form answers and ask what is behind them.
  2. Scope (10 min): restate what is included and what is not. This is the cheapest moment to catch a misunderstanding.
  3. Timeline (10 min): confirm the milestones and the dates you need feedback by.
  4. Working together (10 min): communication, feedback rounds, who approves.
  5. Next steps (5 min): what you will deliver first, and when.

Send a short recap email the same day. It becomes the written record of what you agreed.

Step 5: Set expectations in writing

Most client friction comes from unspoken assumptions. Write the rules down once, in the welcome message, the kickoff recap or a short "how we work" note, and refer back to them. Cover:

  • Communication: which channel (email, not text messages), and how fast you reply. "Within one working day" is a promise you can keep.
  • Working hours: when you are available, and that you do not answer on weekends.
  • Feedback rounds: how many are included (two is common), how long the client has to respond (say, five working days), and that one person gathers feedback from their team.
  • Timeline: the milestone dates, and what happens if feedback is late: the schedule moves by the same amount.
  • Extra requests: how changes outside the scope are quoted and billed.

None of this should be new: it should match the contract, in plain language.

Your onboarding checklist

Copy this and run through it for every new client:

  • Contract signed and deposit invoice sent (or paid, for new clients).
  • Welcome message sent the same day, with one form link, one booking link and one portal link.
  • Onboarding form received: contacts, approver, goals, assets.
  • Access granted through user invites, not passwords by email.
  • Kickoff call held, recap email sent the same day.
  • Communication, feedback rounds and timeline written down.
  • Client portal shared with the contract, invoice, form and meeting.
  • Project set to in progress, first milestone date in your calendar.

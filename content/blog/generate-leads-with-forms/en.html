Most freelancers don't have a lead problem so much as a leak problem. Work comes in through a referral, a portfolio visit or an old client, and then it trickles away: a vague email you answer three days late, a "let's chat sometime" that never becomes a call, a promising inquiry buried under invoices. Getting more leads matters. Getting better ones, and keeping hold of them, matters more.

Start with an offer people can repeat

Referrals only work if the people referring you know what to say. "Lucas does design" is hard to pass on. "Lucas rebrands small B2B software companies in six weeks, usually for €4,800 to €9,000" is easy. Someone hears "we need a new brand before our launch" and your name comes to mind.

Write your offer in one sentence that covers three things:

  • Who it's for: a type of client, not "anyone who needs a website".
  • What they get: a result they recognise, like a launch-ready site or a brand system.
  • Roughly how long and how much: a range is fine. It filters out people who were never going to hire you.

Put that sentence at the top of your website, in your email signature and on your LinkedIn profile. It does more lead generation than any post you'll write this month.

Ask for referrals at the right moment

Most freelancers either never ask for referrals or ask at the worst time, in the final invoice email when the client is thinking about money. The best moment is when the client is happiest: right after a launch, a strong piece of feedback, or the first week a new site brings in sales.

Keep the ask specific and easy to act on:

Really glad the launch went well. If you know another founder who's about to go through a rebrand, I'd love an introduction. I'm taking on two new projects for March.

Specific beats generic ("who's about to go through a rebrand" rather than "anyone who needs design"), and a real constraint ("two projects for March") gives people a reason to act now. Do this with every happy client and you'll build a referral habit that doesn't depend on luck.

Show the work, and the outcome

A portfolio of pretty screenshots tells a visitor you have taste. A case study tells them you can solve their problem. You don't need many. Two or three short ones covering the kind of work you want more of are enough. For each, answer four questions:

  1. What was the client's situation before?
  2. What did you do, and how long did it take?
  3. What changed afterwards (sign-ups, sales, time saved, a funding round closed)?
  4. What would a similar project cost, roughly?

Then end every case study with the same next step: a link to your inquiry form or your booking page. A visitor who just read about a project like theirs is the warmest lead you'll get. Don't make them hunt for your email address.

Replace "contact me" with a form that qualifies

A bare "contact me" link gets you emails like "Hi, how much for a website?" and then three rounds of back-and-forth before you know if the project is a fit. A good inquiry form does that first conversation for you. It asks the questions you'd ask anyway:

  • Name, company and email, so you can reply and know who you're talking to.
  • Project type, as a choice (brand, website, both, something else) rather than a blank box.
  • What they need help with, in their own words.
  • Budget. People are more honest with a number field or ranges than you'd expect, and it saves you a call with someone who has €500 for a €5,000 job.
  • Timeline, as a target date.
  • How they heard about you, so you learn which channels actually work.

Seven or eight questions is plenty. Longer forms lose people. Shorter ones leave you guessing. A form like this does slightly lower your number of inquiries, and that's fine. The ones you get are clearer, and a "no" now is cheaper than a "no" after a call and a proposal.

Answer fast

A lead who fills in your form today is often filling in two or three others. The first freelancer to reply with something useful usually gets the call. Aim to answer within one working day, ideally the same day.

Fast doesn't mean a full proposal. A good first reply is short:

  • Thank them and repeat back what you understood, in one or two sentences.
  • Say whether the budget and timeline look workable, honestly.
  • Offer a 20 to 30 minute call with a booking link, so nobody has to trade five emails about times.

Review it once a week. Anything sitting in the same stage for more than a week needs a nudge or an honest close. The "lost" notes are worth more than they look: after a few months they tell you whether you lose on price, on timing or on fit.

A weekly lead routine

Lead generation works best as a small habit rather than a panic when the calendar empties. Thirty minutes a week is enough:

  • Reply to any new inquiry still waiting.
  • Follow up on proposals older than five days.
  • Ask one happy client for a referral or a testimonial.
  • Update one case study or write a new one.
  • Check "how did you hear about us" answers and do more of what works.

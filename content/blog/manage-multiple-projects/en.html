One client project is easy. Four at once is where things slip: a reply you meant to send on Tuesday, a deadline you remembered on Thursday, a deposit you forgot to chase. The fix is rarely working harder. It is a small system that keeps every project visible, gives each one a clear next step, and tells you early when something is about to go wrong.

Keep one list of every project

If your projects live in your head, your inbox and three sticky notes, you will drop something. The first rule is simple: every active project goes on one list, and only one.

For each project, write down four things:

  • The client and the project name. "Juniper: brand identity", not "logo thing".
  • The stage. Where it is right now, in a word or two.
  • The next step. One concrete action, with a verb: "Send round 2 concepts", "Wait for copy from Sam".
  • The date. When the project, or its next milestone, is due.

Keep the stages few. Four or five is plenty for most freelancers: Planned, In progress, Waiting on client, Completed, Cancelled. Each stage should answer one question: whose move is it? If a project is "in progress", the move is yours. If it is "waiting on client", it is theirs, and your job is to follow up on a date, not to forget about it.

Use a board to see the whole picture

A list sorted by due date tells you what is urgent. A board tells you where your work is piling up. Put one column per stage and one card per project, and the shape of your week becomes obvious. Six cards in "In progress" and nothing in "Planned" means you are busy now and empty next month. Four cards in "Waiting on client" means you should send some nudges today.

Use both views. The board for the weekly picture, the list sorted by date for the daily one.

Give every project a next step and a date

Most dropped balls are projects with no next step written down. They sit there looking fine until the client emails to ask what is happening.

A good next step is small, specific and has an owner:

  • Weak: "Website". Better: "Build the contact page template".
  • Weak: "Feedback". Better: "Chase Sam for homepage feedback on Thursday".
  • Weak: "Invoice". Better: "Send the milestone invoice after concepts are approved".

Each project also needs a date you believe in. Not the date the client hoped for in the first call, but the one you agreed after scoping. If a project has no date, ask for one or set your own internal target. A project without a date always loses to one that has a date.

Plan the week, not just the day

Once a week, usually Friday afternoon or Monday morning, spend 20 minutes on the whole list. Go through every project and ask:

  1. Is the stage still right?
  2. Is the next step still the right one?
  3. Is the date still realistic?
  4. What do I need from the client this week?

Then block time in your calendar for the work itself. Give your biggest project two or three half-day blocks, not scattered hours between calls. Switching between three clients in one morning costs more than it looks: every switch means reloading the files, the feedback and the context.

Keep the money side of the week short and separate: check what is unpaid and what time is unbilled, then get back to the work. That routine deserves its own article, so we won't go deep on it here.

Know how many projects you can really take

Capacity is the part freelancers most often guess. Here is a quick way to check it with real numbers.

Say you work 40 hours a week. About 12 go to email, calls, admin and sales, so you have roughly 28 hours for client work. Your projects need:

  • Juniper brand identity (€4,800, about 60 hours over 6 weeks): 10 hours a week.
  • Orbit website (€7,200, about 90 hours over 8 weeks): 11 hours a week.
  • Two small retainers: 3 hours a week each.

That is 27 hours. You are full. A fifth project now means late nights or late deliveries, even if it looks small. The honest options are to start it in three weeks, raise the price for a rush, or say no.

Your hour estimates should come from tracked time, not hope. If brand projects keep taking you 60 hours when you planned 40, plan with 60. Our guide to time tracking for freelancers shows how to get those numbers, and how to price freelance work covers what to do once you have them.

Keep each project's things together

Juggling gets hard when the pieces of one project are spread out: the proposal in one folder, the contract in your downloads, the invoices in a spreadsheet, the hours in another app and the feedback buried in email. Every question ("Did they approve round 2?", "Has the deposit been paid?") turns into a search.

Keep the proposal, contract, invoices, tracked time and client messages attached to the project they belong to. When a client calls, you open one page and have the answer. When you share things with the client, one place for them helps too; that is what client portals are for.

Tell clients early when a date slips

Dates slip. Feedback arrives late, a scope grows, you get sick. Clients rarely mind a delay they hear about early. They mind hearing about it the day before.

The rule: as soon as you know a date will move, tell the client. Say what changed, give the new date, and say what you need from them, if anything.

Subject: Orbit website: updated timeline

Hi Sam,

A quick update on the website. The homepage copy arrived a week later than planned, so the launch date moves from 24 October to 31 October. Everything else is on track.

To keep the new date, I need your feedback on the page templates by Monday 20 October.

Thanks,
Alex

Short, factual, with a new date and a clear ask. If the delay comes from extra requests, say so plainly and point to the agreed scope.

A ten-minute start to the day

A daily routine keeps the weekly plan honest. Before you open your design tool or code editor:

Start-of-day checklist
1. Open your project list, sorted by due date.
2. Check anything due this week. Is it on track?
3. Answer client emails that block a project.
4. Follow up on anything "waiting on client" for more than three days.
5. Pick the one or two projects you will work on today, and start a timer.
6. If a date is at risk, send the update now, not tonight.

That's ten minutes. Then do the work.

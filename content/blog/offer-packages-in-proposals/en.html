When you send a client one price, you are asking a yes-or-no question. When you send three well-built packages, you are asking "which one fits you best?" That small change makes it less likely the client walks away or haggles, and more likely they spend a little more than they planned. This guide covers how to design freelance packages that sell, then shows how to send them in a proposal the client can choose from and accept online.

Why packages work better than a single quote

A single quote invites comparison with everyone else the client has asked. A set of packages invites comparison with your own options. The client's attention moves from "is this freelancer too expensive?" to "do I need the brand guide or not?"

  • Fewer negotiations. If the price feels high, the client can step down a tier instead of asking you for a discount. You keep your rate and change the scope.
  • Higher average project value. A good share of clients choose the middle or top option when it is clearly worth it.
  • Faster proposals. Once your packages are defined, most quotes are a matter of adjusting a few lines.
  • Clearer scope. Each tier lists what is included, so what is not included is visible too.

Packages don't replace pricing from your own numbers. Work out your minimum rate first (our guide on how to price your freelance work walks through it), then make sure even your smallest package sits comfortably above it.

Good, better, best: what goes in each tier

The classic structure is three tiers. Each one should be a complete, sensible project on its own, not a crippled version designed to push the client upward.

The entry package

The smallest version of the outcome the client actually needs. It solves the core problem with fewer extras and fewer revision rounds. It exists for clients with a tight budget, and so that the next tier looks like a clear step up.

The middle package

The one you expect most clients to choose, and the one you would recommend if they asked. Build it first, from what a typical good project looks like for you, then derive the other two from it.

The top package

Everything in the middle tier plus the things that make the result go further: more deliverables, a launch kit, extra revision rounds, priority turnaround, a month of support. Some clients will choose it. For the rest, it makes the middle option look reasonable.

Notice the steps: about €1,500 between each tier, and each step adds something the client can picture. A jump that is too small makes the top tier an easy upgrade you may not have priced well. A jump that is too big makes it feel unreachable.

Anchoring without games

People judge prices by what they see next to them. A €5,400 option on the same page makes €3,900 feel moderate. That is anchoring, and it works without any pressure or tricks, as long as every option is real.

  • List the top package first or last, consistently. Many freelancers put the entry package first so the order reads like a ladder. Either way, the client sees all three at once.
  • Only offer what you would happily deliver. If someone picks the top tier, you should be pleased, not worried.
  • Don't fake scarcity or discounts. "Only two slots left this month" works once and costs trust afterwards.

Naming your packages

Names do more work than they seem to. "Basic" sounds like something you would be embarrassed to choose. "Premium" sounds like a markup. Good names describe the level of the outcome, not the size of the bill.

  • Describe depth: Essential, Signature, Complete, or Starter, Growth, Full launch.
  • Keep them short and in the same style, so the three read as a set.
  • Add one sentence under each name about who it is for: "For teams that need a refreshed mark before a product launch."

How many packages to offer

Three is the default for good reason: enough to compare, not so many that the client stalls. Two works when the projects are simple, for example "with or without copywriting". Four or more usually means you are listing variations that should be add-ons instead. If a client wants one extra item, quote it as a separate line or a small follow-up, rather than inventing a fourth tier.

Before you send, run this quick check:

  • Each package is a complete project with its own deliverables.
  • Each step up adds something the client can see and name.
  • The number of revision rounds is stated in every tier.
  • The cheapest package is still above your minimum rate.
  • Timeline and payment terms are the same for all, or the differences are spelled out.

Sending packages in Thrive, step by step

Thrive has a proposal template built for exactly this. Here is how the brand refresh above goes from blank page to accepted package.

1. Start from the Package proposal template

Under Documents, create a new proposal. The New proposal dialog offers Simple proposal, Project proposal, Package proposal, Services proposal and Blank proposal. Pick Package proposal ("Let the client choose a package"), choose the client and, if you have one, the project. The template starts with placeholders for the client's goal, the differences between your packages, deliverables and payment terms.

2. Write the content

The editor has four steps: Content, Pricing, Design and Review. In Content, summarise the client's goal in their own words, then explain in a few lines how the packages differ. Keep the detail for the pricing table.

3. Build the packages in Pricing

With the Pricing table turned on, choose how the client selects:

  • Single option: one list of included services, no choice.
  • Packages: the client chooses one package.
  • Multi-select: the client chooses several packages.

For good, better, best, choose Packages. Each package has a title, a short description and its own service lines, each with a unit, rate and quantity. You can type lines in directly or add services from your catalog under Services & packages. Each package shows its subtotal, an optional tax percentage and its total, so you can check that Essential, Signature and Complete land on €2,400, €3,900 and €5,400.

Prices from the catalog are copied into the proposal, so changing your catalog later never changes a proposal you already sent.

4. Design, review and publish

In Design, pick a layout, your logo, accent colour and title font. Review shows a summary (for example "3 packages") before you publish. Publishing creates a private link to send to the client.

5. The client chooses and accepts

The client opens the link and sees Choose your package, with each package's description, services and total. They pick one and accept. Thrive checks the selection and the total on the server, then records exactly which package they chose and at what price. If the proposal is linked to a project with project billing on, the chosen package's total becomes the project fee, and the first payment in the billing schedule becomes the deposit invoice.

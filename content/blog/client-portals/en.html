Your client is looking for the contract you sent three weeks ago. Was it in the email with the proposal, or the one after the call? Is the PDF they found the signed version or the draft? A client portal ends that search. It is one private page where a client finds everything they need from you, and it makes a studio of one or two people feel organized in a way clients notice.

The real cost of scattered attachments

Email works fine for conversation. It works badly as a filing system. On a typical €4,800 brand project, a client might receive a proposal, a revised proposal, a contract, a deposit invoice, a questionnaire, two milestone invoices and a handful of meeting invites. That is ten or more emails, spread across two months and sometimes two or three people on their side.

The cost shows up in small ways:

  • "Which version is the latest?" Two proposal PDFs with similar names, and the client signs the wrong one.
  • Lost invoices. The deposit invoice went to your contact, but their finance team pays. Nobody can find it, so payment slips a week.
  • Repeated questions. "Can you resend the bank details?" "When is our next call?" Each one takes five minutes, and they add up.
  • A slightly messy impression. Clients rarely complain about it, but a pile of attachments feels less professional than a single, tidy page.

Bigger agencies solve this with project managers and client dashboards. You can get most of the benefit with one link.

What clients actually need to see

A portal is not a window into your whole workspace. It is a short list of what the client needs to act on or keep. For most projects, that is:

  • Proposals and contracts, in their current version, with a clear note when something needs their acceptance or signature.
  • Invoices, with the amount outstanding and how to pay.
  • Forms you need them to fill in, such as a project questionnaire or content request.
  • Project status: where things are and what comes next.
  • Upcoming meetings, with the time and location.

The most useful part is the order. Things that need the client's attention should come first: a contract to sign, an unpaid invoice, a form to complete. Everything else is reference.

What to share, and what to keep private

A good rule: share what the client has already agreed to see, or needs to act on. Keep your working material to yourself.

Share: final or published documents, issued invoices, requested forms, the project status, scheduled meetings.

Keep private: drafts, internal notes about the client, your budget and margin on the project, meeting prep notes, and anything you are still discussing with a collaborator. If a document is not ready for the client to read and respond to, it does not belong in the portal yet.

It also helps to be clear about what a portal is not. It is a place to find and respond to things. Conversation, feedback rounds and quick questions still happen by email or on calls, and that is fine.

Keep it private and simple

Clients do not want another account. Every extra password is a reason for them to skip the portal and email you instead. The simplest approach is a private link: they click it, and they are in.

The trade-off is that a private link works for whoever has it, like a shared document link. That is usually what you want (the client can forward it to their finance person), but it means you should be able to control it: set an expiry if the project is short, and switch it off when the work ends or the link went to the wrong person.

How the client portal works in Thrive

In Thrive, each client can have one portal that spans all of their projects. You configure it from the client (or one of their projects) by choosing Client portal.

Set your defaults once

In Settings, under Client portal, you choose a default portal title, a welcome message and which sections are Shared by default: Documents, Forms, Invoices, Projects and Meetings. Every new client portal starts from these settings.

Choose exactly what each client sees

Inside a client's portal, you can change the title and welcome message, switch each section on or off, and then pick the exact records to share. Thrive is strict about this, which is what you want:

  • Nothing is shared until you select it. A new proposal or invoice stays private until you add it to the portal.
  • Only finished items can be selected: published document versions and issued invoices. Drafts never appear.
  • Documents are pinned to a version. If you revise a proposal, the client keeps seeing the version you shared until you select the new one. That is how you avoid "which version is the latest?" on the client's side.
  • Internal data stays out. Internal notes, custom fields, project budgets and meeting briefs are never sent to the portal, even when the related record is shared.

Before sharing, Client preview shows you the page exactly as the client will see it, with responses disabled so you cannot accept your own proposal by accident.

What the client can do

Pending documents, requested forms and unpaid invoices are listed first. From the portal, the client can review and accept a proposal, sign a contract with a typed-name signature, fill in forms and attach files, download documents and invoices, see your payment instructions and check the project status. Selected meetings show their time and location.

When you record a payment or the client completes a response, the pending list updates on its own. Clients cannot mark an invoice as paid themselves. Thrive does not take payments either: the invoice shows the bank details or other instructions you wrote, and the client pays you directly. Links, expiry and switching it off

Turn on Enable client portal, then Save & create link. You choose how long the link lasts (7, 30 or 90 days, or no expiry) and copy it to send. You can revoke the link at any time and create a new one later.

There are a few ways to stop access. Revoking the link blocks it. Turning the portal off blocks it too. Removing an item or hiding a section takes it out of the portal immediately, and archiving the client closes the portal. One thing to know: if you also sent a separate link for a single document or invoice, that link has its own settings and keeps working until you revoke it there.

To be clear about the limits: Thrive portals do not add client accounts, messaging, booking or payment collection. They are a clean, private page for what you choose to share.

Introduce the portal at kickoff

A portal only helps if the client uses it. The best moment to introduce it is right after they accept the proposal, when you send the contract or the first invoice. Set expectations once, and the habit sticks.

Here is a short email you can copy:

Subject: Your project page for the brand identity

Hi Sam,

Thanks again for going ahead with the project. To keep things simple, everything you need from us will live on one private page: the contract, invoices, the questionnaire and our meeting times.

Here is your link: [link]. No login needed. Anything waiting for you is listed at the top. Right now that is the contract to sign and the deposit invoice.

Feel free to share the link with whoever handles payments on your side. We will still talk by email and on calls as usual.

Best,
Alex

Note the last line. Telling the client they can forward it to finance solves the most common reason invoices go unpaid.

Keep it current

A portal that is out of date is worse than none, because the client stops trusting it. Make updating it part of the moments you already have:

  • When you publish a document (or a new version), add it to the portal.
  • When you issue an invoice, add it before you send the reminder email.
  • When you book a meeting, add it so the time and place are in one spot.
  • When you need input, assign the form there instead of attaching a questionnaire.
  • When the project ends, decide whether to leave the portal on for downloads or turn it off.

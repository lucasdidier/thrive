A deposit is the simplest protection a freelancer has. It turns "sounds great, let's go" into a real commitment, covers you if the project stalls, and means you are never weeks of work out of pocket. Yet many freelancers still start on a promise, because asking for money before doing anything feels awkward. It shouldn't. This guide covers why deposits matter, how much to ask for and when, how to split larger projects into milestones, and what to write in your terms.

Why take a deposit at all

Clients rarely mean to pay late or disappear. But a project without a deposit has no cost for the client to pause, and every cost for you.

  • It confirms the commitment. A client who pays a deposit has decided. A client who hasn't may still be comparing quotes.
  • It protects your calendar. When you book a project, you turn other work away. The deposit pays for that reserved time if the client cancels.
  • It covers your costs. Stock images, fonts, prints, travel or subcontractors are paid before the client ever sees the result.
  • It filters clients. Clients who push back hard on any deposit are often the same ones who push back on the final invoice.

Professional clients expect it. Agencies, architects and builders all ask for money up front. You are not asking for a favour.

Typical deposit percentages, and when to use each

There is no single right number. The deposit should match the size of the project, how long it runs and how well you know the client.

DepositGood forExample
25–30%Large, long projects split into several milestones; trusted repeat clients€12,000 website: €3,000 up front, then three milestones
50%Most fixed-price projects up to a few thousand euros€3,900 brand refresh: €1,950 on acceptance, €1,950 on delivery
100%Small jobs, short sessions, new clients you can't assess, or work that is hard to "take back"€450 photo session or a €900 workshop day, paid in full before the date

50% is the sensible default. It is common enough that few clients question it, and it means that if the project stops halfway, you have been paid for roughly the work done.

Go lower when the project is large, but don't drop the up-front payment altogether. Replace it with a schedule, so you never carry more than a month or so of unpaid work.

Ask for 100% when the job is small or the date is fixed. Chasing the second half of a €400 invoice costs more time than it is worth. And for a booked day, a shoot or an event, the time can't be resold once the date has passed.

Milestone schedules for bigger projects

For projects that run longer than a month, split the fee into payments tied to clear points in the work. The client pays as they see progress, and you are never far ahead of the money.

A €12,000 website might look like this:

  1. Deposit, 25%: €3,000 on acceptance.
  2. Design approved, 25%: €3,000.
  3. Development complete, 25%: €3,000.
  4. Launch, 25%: €3,000.

Good milestones are moments the client can recognise: "design approved", not "60% complete". Tie each payment to an event, and say in your terms that the next phase starts when the previous invoice is paid. That one sentence keeps a schedule from drifting.

Refundable or non-refundable?

Most freelancers make the deposit non-refundable, because it pays for reserved time and early work that can't be undone. Say so clearly, and say what happens if the project is cancelled later. This is general guidance, not legal advice: consumer rules differ by country, so if you work with private individuals, check what applies where you are.

Some wording you can adapt:

A deposit of 50% of the project fee is due on acceptance of this proposal. Work is scheduled once the deposit is received. The deposit is non-refundable, as it covers reserved time and initial work. If the project is cancelled after the first concepts are delivered, 75% of the fee is due. Each later payment is due within 14 days of its invoice, and the next phase starts when the previous payment has been received.

What to put in your terms

Whatever the percentage, your proposal or contract should answer these questions before the first invoice exists:

  • How much is the deposit, as a percentage and an amount?
  • When is it due, and does work start only after it arrives?
  • Is it refundable, and in which cases?
  • What are the later payments, and what triggers each one?
  • What are the payment terms for each invoice (for example net 14)?
  • What happens if a payment is late: does work pause?
  • How will the client pay: bank transfer, a payment link, something else?

Put the schedule in the proposal itself, not only in a separate email. When the client accepts, they accept the price and the way it will be billed at the same time. Our guide to proposals and contracts covers the rest of what belongs in those documents.

Set your default deposit once

Under Settings → Proposals, the New proposals section has Deposit (%), a whole number from 1 to 100. It starts at 50. Next to it you set the default client response and Standard terms added to every new proposal, which is a good place for the deposit wording above.

Turn on project billing in the proposal

Create the proposal from a project, so it is linked to it. In the editor's Pricing step, turn on Project billing: "Create a deposit invoice when this document is accepted or signed." Then set the Project fee and the Billing schedule. Thrive fills the schedule from your default: with 50%, that is a Deposit row and a Final payment row.

You can rename rows, change percentages and use Add payment for milestones, up to eight after the deposit. The first row is always paid On acceptance or signature; later rows are marked Issue from the project. The percentages must add up to 100%. If you use a package pricing table, the schedule is applied to the total of the package the client picks (see offering packages in proposals).

A few honest limits. Project billing needs a linked project, visible pricing and fixed fees. Time-and-materials pricing is an estimate, so it doesn't create deposit or milestone invoices automatically; you invoice actual hours instead. A project has one billing agreement, so a second proposal for the same project won't create invoices.

The client sees the schedule before they accept

On the proposal link, the client sees a Fee & payment schedule section: the total fee, each payment with its amount, and whether it is due on acceptance or issued later. Before they accept, a note tells them the deposit invoice will appear in their client portal and that no payment is collected on the page.

The deposit invoice is created on acceptance

When the client accepts or signs, Thrive creates the deposit invoice from the first row of the schedule, with your invoice layout and payment instructions. If you leave the notification on, you get an email at the same time. Later payments are issued from the project when you reach each milestone, and Thrive won't let invoices from the schedule go over the agreed fee.

Thrive does not process payments. The invoice shows the payment instructions you write, such as your bank details or your own payment link, and the client pays you directly.

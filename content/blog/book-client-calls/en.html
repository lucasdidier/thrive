"Does Tuesday at 3 work?" "I'm out Tuesday, how about Thursday morning?" "Thursday I have a dentist appointment, maybe next week?" Four emails later, you have a 30-minute call booked and a lead who has gone a little cold. Scheduling ping-pong looks harmless, but it costs you clients and focus. Here is how to set up client calls so people book a time in one step, and so those calls stay short and useful.

Why ping-pong scheduling costs you more than time

Every round of "what works for you?" adds a day. For a warm lead who just filled in your inquiry form, a day is a long time. They are often talking to two or three other freelancers, and the one who makes it easy to talk tends to get the first conversation.

It also breaks your concentration. Each scheduling email is a small interruption, and five calls a week means dozens of them chopping up your deep work.

The fix is simple: decide once when you take calls, then give people a link where they can pick a free slot themselves.

Decide when you take calls

Before you share any link, write down your rules. A booking page only protects your time if the rules behind it are good.

Working hours and days

You don't have to offer your whole working day. Many freelancers keep calls to a block, such as Tuesday to Thursday, 1 pm to 5 pm, and keep mornings for focused work.

Minimum notice

Without a notice period, someone can book a call that starts in 20 minutes, in the middle of a design session. Set a minimum: 4 hours if you like to move fast, 24 hours if you want to prepare properly. For a discovery call, 24 hours gives you time to look at the client's website and their inquiry before you talk.

Buffers between calls

Back-to-back calls are where meetings run over and notes never get written. A 15-minute gap after each call gives you time to write down what was said, and to stretch your legs. If a call runs five minutes long, the next client doesn't pay for it.

How far ahead

Limit how far into the future people can book. Two to four weeks is usually enough.

One call type per purpose

Not every call is the same. A stranger asking about a website needs something different from a client you have worked with for a year. Set up a few call types, each with its own length, description and location:

  • Discovery call (30 minutes): for new leads. Goals, budget range, timeline, whether you are a good fit.
  • Project kickoff (60 minutes): after the contract is signed. Access, contacts, milestones, how you will work together.
  • Check-in (15 or 30 minutes): for active projects. Review progress, decide next steps.

Give each a short description, so the client knows what the call is for. "A 30-minute call about your project, budget and timeline" beats "Call with Alex".

Share the right link at the right moment. The discovery link goes in your inquiry reply or on your website. The kickoff link goes out once the contract is signed (our guide to proposals and contracts covers that part). Check-in links go only to current clients.

Protect your deep-work time

A booking link is a promise that your calendar is honest. Keep it honest:

  • Block focus time in your calendar like a meeting. If it is in your calendar, it is not free for calls.
  • Keep personal appointments in the same calendar, or connected to it, so they block slots too.
  • Cap your call days. If you keep calls to three afternoons a week, two days stay completely free.

Time zones without the math

Time zones cause more missed calls than anything else. "Let's talk at 4" means nothing when one of you is in Lisbon and the other in New York.

A booking page solves most of it, because the slot is a fixed moment in both calendars. A few habits help too. Always write the time zone in emails ("Thursday, 4 pm Lisbon time, 11 am New York"). Set your call hours with distant clients in mind: if half your clients are in North America, an afternoon block in Europe overlaps with their morning. And watch the weeks when clocks change, since Europe and the US switch on different dates.

Before the call: send an agenda

A short agenda turns a chat into a meeting with an outcome. Send it the day before, or put it in the event type's description so the client sees it while booking.

Subject: Our call on Thursday, 4 pm (Lisbon time)

Hi Sam,

Looking forward to Thursday. Here is what I'd like to cover in our 30 minutes:

1. What you want the new website to achieve
2. Must-have pages and features
3. Timeline and budget range
4. Next steps

If you have examples of sites you like, send them over before the call.

The link to join: [link]

Alex

For discovery calls, ask a few questions up front so you arrive prepared. An inquiry form does this well: the client describes the project before they ever book. See how in generating leads with forms.

After the call: send a recap the same day

The recap is where calls turn into work. Send it within a few hours, while both of you still remember the details. Keep it to what was decided, what happens next and who does it.

When clients don't show up

No-shows happen, usually because of a busy day, not bad intent. Handle them calmly:

  1. Wait 10 minutes, then send a short, friendly message: "I'm on the call, are you still able to join?"
  2. After 15 minutes, end it and send your booking link: "No problem, here is the link to pick another time."
  3. Second no-show from a lead? Don't chase. Leave the link with them and move on.

Prevention helps more than any policy: a clear description and an agenda the day before make a call feel real. For paid sessions, your contract can set a cancellation notice.

A quick setup checklist

  • Call days and hours chosen, with focus time kept free.
  • Minimum notice set (4 to 24 hours).
  • A buffer after each call (10 to 15 minutes).
  • Booking window limited to a few weeks.
  • One call type per purpose, each with a clear description and location.
  • Your calendar connected, with personal appointments in it.
  • An agenda template and a recap template ready to reuse.

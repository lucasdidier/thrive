A client asks, "Did you get the logo files I sent?" You know you did. You search for their name, then their company, then "logo", then scroll through three threads with nearly identical subjects. Five minutes later you find it, buried under a newsletter and a calendar invite. None of this is hard work, but it happens a dozen times a week, and it's the reason so many freelancers feel busy without getting much done.

What hunting through your inbox really costs

An inbox is sorted by time, not by client. That's fine for a job with one employer. For a freelancer with six active clients, it means every client's conversation is interleaved with every other client's, plus everything else that lands in your mailbox.

The cost shows up in small ways:

  • Lost context. Before a call, you piece together the project from scattered emails instead of reading one history.
  • Missed replies. A client's question gets read on your phone, marked as read, and forgotten. Four days later they follow up, a bit less warm than before.
  • Scope arguments. "We agreed on two rounds of revisions" is easy to prove when you can find the email where you agreed it. It's much harder when you can't.
  • Switching cost. Every trip into the inbox to find one thing pulls you past twenty other things that want your attention.

Say you lose ten minutes a day to searching and re-reading. That's around 40 hours a year, a full working week, at whatever your hourly rate is. At €80 an hour, that's €3,200 of time spent looking for things you already have.

A simple system that works in any inbox

You don't need special software to get most of the benefit. You need three habits.

1. One thread per topic

Start a new email when the subject changes, and give it a subject that says what it's about. "Northwind website: homepage feedback, round 2" will still make sense in six months. "Re: Re: Fwd: quick question" won't. When a client replies to an old thread with a new topic, answer in a fresh thread with a clear subject and say so: "Starting a new thread for the invoice question so it doesn't get lost."

This sounds fussy, but it pays back quickly. Each thread becomes a record of one decision or one piece of work, which is exactly what you need when you look back.

2. A reply status you decide on purpose

"Unread" is a poor signal. You read emails on your phone between meetings without being able to answer them. What you really need to know is: does this one need a reply from me? Decide that once, when you read it, and mark it. Either it needs a reply, or it doesn't. The emails that need replies are your to-do list for the next time you sit down at your desk.

A good rule: answer anything that needs a reply within one working day, even if the answer is "I'll get back to you on Thursday with the revised timeline."

3. Archive when it's done

Once a conversation is finished, archive it. Not delete: archive. The history stays searchable, but it stops competing for your attention. A short list of open conversations is far easier to manage than a long one where finished and unfinished look the same.

A quick daily email routine

  • Go through new client emails once in the morning and once mid-afternoon, not all day.
  • For each one: reply now if it takes under two minutes, mark it as needing a reply if it doesn't, or archive it if nothing is needed.
  • Before you stop for the day, check that nothing marked as needing a reply is older than a day.

What it doesn't do

It's worth being clear about the limits so you know when to open your normal mail app:

  • Attachments stay in your mailbox. Thrive shows the conversation text, not the files.
  • Emails are shown as plain text, without remote images.
  • Only recent conversations with clients are brought in, with the latest messages of each thread. Older messages stay in your mailbox.
  • Refresh is manual, so press Refresh before relying on the list.

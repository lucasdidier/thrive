Most late payments are not bad clients. They are unclear invoices, missing payment details, a due date nobody agreed to, or an email that got buried. You can fix almost all of that before the work even starts. Here is how to invoice clients so getting paid becomes the boring, predictable part of freelancing.

Agree on payment before you start the work

The best time to talk about money is before the project begins, when everyone is optimistic and nothing is owed yet. Put the payment terms in your proposal or contract, not in a surprise line at the bottom of the first invoice.

For fixed-price work, a payment schedule protects both sides. A common one for a €4,800 brand project:

  • 50% deposit (€2,400) when the client accepts or signs, before work starts.
  • 25% milestone (€1,200) when the first concepts are delivered.
  • 25% final payment (€1,200) on delivery of the final files.

Smaller jobs can be 50/50. Longer engagements can be monthly. What matters is that the client has seen and agreed to the schedule, the due terms (net 14, net 30) and how they will pay. When the first invoice arrives, it should be exactly what they expected.

A deposit does more than cover your costs. It confirms the client is serious, and it means you never work for weeks on credit. If you are unsure how to write these terms, our guide to proposals and contracts covers it step by step.

What a good invoice contains

Invoicing rules differ by country, and some places have specific requirements (tax numbers, wording for VAT exemptions, how long you keep records). Check with your local tax office or an accountant. In general terms, though, a clear invoice includes:

  • Your details: name or business name, address, and any registration or tax number you are required to show.
  • The client's details: company name, contact and address.
  • A unique invoice number. Use one sequence, with no gaps or reuse: INV-001, INV-002, INV-003. Clients' accounts teams rely on it, and so will you at tax time.
  • Issue date and due date. Write the actual due date ("Due 24 November"), not just "net 14". People act on dates.
  • Line items: what you did, the quantity (hours, days, items), the rate and the amount. Name the project so nobody has to guess.
  • Totals: subtotal, tax if it applies, the total due, and any amount already paid.
  • Payment instructions: bank details (account holder, IBAN or account number, BIC or routing number), any other accepted method, and the reference to use.

That last point is the one freelancers most often get wrong. If the client has to email you to ask how to pay, you have added a week. Ask them to use the invoice number as the payment reference, so you can match the payment when it lands.

Fixed price or tracked time

How you fill in the line items depends on how you priced the work.

Fixed price

Invoice against the schedule you agreed. Each invoice names its part of the project: "Brand identity: deposit (50%)", then "Brand identity: milestone 1 (25%)", and so on. The client can see where they are in the project and what is left.

Hourly or daily

Time-based invoices need clean records. Log time as you work, with a short note per entry, and mark what is billable. At the end of the week or month, bill the unbilled entries for that project. Group them in a way the client can read: by day, by task, or one line per entry if they like detail.

Two habits help a lot. Keep each entry's rate as it was when you logged it, so a rate change later does not quietly rewrite old work. And never bill the same hour twice: once time is on an invoice, it should be locked. If you are setting this up, read our guide to time tracking for freelancers.

Many projects mix both: a fixed fee for the agreed scope, plus hourly billing for extra requests. Say so in your contract, then show extra time as a separate invoice or clearly labeled lines, so it never looks like the price went up without warning.

Follow up on overdue invoices without the awkwardness

Chasing payment feels personal. It is not. Your client's accounts team handles dozens of invoices, and a polite reminder is a normal part of that process. The awkward part is waiting three weeks and then writing an annoyed email.

A simple rhythm that works:

  1. On the due date: nothing, or a short "just checking this reached the right person" if it is a new client.
  2. 3 days late: a friendly reminder with the invoice link.
  3. 10 days late: a second reminder. Ask if anything is blocking payment, such as a purchase order number or a different contact.
  4. 20+ days late: a phone call or a firmer email, and pause new work until the balance is settled, as your contract says.

Here is a short reminder you can copy:

Subject: Reminder: INV-014 is overdue

Hi Sam,

A friendly reminder that INV-014 was due on 24 November. The outstanding balance is €1,200.

Payment details are on the invoice: [link]. Please use INV-014 as the payment reference.

Thank you,
Alex

Keep it short, factual and kind. No apologies, no guilt. Include the number, the date, the amount and the link.

You read it, adjust it if you want, and send it. If you turn on the daily or weekly summary email, overdue invoices are listed there too, so you do not have to remember to check.

Keep everything tied to the client and project

When an invoice is connected to its project, answering questions gets easy. How much of this project has been paid? Which hours have not been billed yet? Did they sign the contract with net 14 or net 30? The answers sit in one place instead of across a spreadsheet, an email thread and a folder of PDFs.

A quick checklist before you send any invoice:

  • The amount matches what the client agreed to in the proposal or contract.
  • The invoice number is the next one in your sequence.
  • The due date is a real date, matching your agreed terms.
  • Payment instructions are complete, with the reference to use.
  • It goes to the person who actually pays, not just your day-to-day contact.

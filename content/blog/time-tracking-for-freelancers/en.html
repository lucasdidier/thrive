If you charge a flat fee, tracking your hours can feel pointless. The client pays €4,800 either way, so why time yourself? Because the fee is fixed but your hours are not. Without a record, you can't tell whether a project paid you well or quietly cost you money. You also have nothing solid to stand on when the scope starts to grow.

Know your real hourly rate on every project

Every fixed price hides an hourly rate. You only find it by dividing the fee by the hours you actually worked.

Here is an example. You quote a brand identity project at €4,800. You expect about 40 hours, so in your head you are charging €120 an hour. Then the project runs like this:

  • Discovery and research: 8 hours
  • Logo concepts: 14 hours
  • Revisions (four rounds instead of two): 18 hours
  • Brand guidelines and file exports: 12 hours
  • Calls and emails: 8 hours

That adds up to 64 hours, and €4,800 divided by 64 is €75 an hour. You gave up €45 an hour, or about €2,880 of the value you planned to earn. Most of it went to revisions and communication, the two things people almost never estimate.

Without tracking you would still feel that the project dragged. With tracking you know where the time went and by how much. That turns a vague feeling into something you can fix.

Spot scope creep while you can still act

Scope creep rarely arrives as one big request. It comes as small ones: "one more logo option", "a quick social banner", "can we hop on a call?" Each one seems too small to raise, and together they cost you days.

Tracked time gives you an early warning. If you have used 32 of your 40 estimated hours and you are still on the second round of concepts, you know long before the end that something is off. At that point you can still:

  • Point back to the scope in your proposal or contract and say what is included.
  • Offer a change: extra revision rounds at your hourly rate, or a revised fixed price.
  • Decide on purpose to absorb the extra work because the client is worth it.

All three are fine. What isn't fine is only noticing after the final invoice that you worked 24 extra hours for free.

Price the next project better

Your time records become your pricing data. After five or six projects of one type you stop guessing. You might learn that a brand identity usually takes you 55 to 65 hours, not 40, and that revisions are always the part that runs over.

So next time you can quote €6,500 instead of €4,800, or keep the price and limit revisions to two rounds with extra rounds billed by the hour. Either way, you set the price from evidence, not hope. If you write proposals with a breakdown of phases, your logged tasks also tell you how long each phase really takes.

Keep records clients trust

On hourly work, your time log is the invoice backup. A client who sees "Tuesday, 2:15, homepage layout revisions" trusts a bill far more than one that just says "Design work: 12 hours".

On fixed-price work, records help when you have to ask for more. "We agreed on two revision rounds. Rounds three and four took 9 hours, here are the entries" is a calm, factual conversation. "This took way longer than expected" is not.

Billable vs non-billable time

Track both, but keep them apart.

  • Billable time is work a client pays for: design, development, client meetings, the extra revisions you agreed to bill.
  • Non-billable time is everything else that keeps your business running: admin, invoicing, sales calls, portfolio updates, learning.

Non-billable time matters because it eats into the hours you can sell. If you work 40 hours a week but 12 go to admin and sales, you have 28 billable hours. Your rates have to cover the whole week, not just the billable part. On fixed-price projects, the split is a choice: log everything on the project so the real rate is accurate, and mark as billable only what you plan to charge on top of the fee.

Simple habits that make tracking stick

The best system is the one you keep using. A few habits help.

Timer or end-of-day log

A running timer is the most accurate, as long as you remember to start and stop it. Logging at the end of the day is easier to keep up, but memory is generous: you tend to forget the small interruptions and the 20-minute calls. Many freelancers use a timer for focused work and add the small things before closing the laptop. Pick one approach and try it for two weeks before you judge it.

Round to 15 minutes

Logging 1:07 and 0:52 is more precision than anyone needs. Rounding to the nearest 15 minutes keeps the log readable and honest. Tell clients in your contract how you round, so it never surprises them.

Write a note on every entry

"Work" is useless six weeks later. "Logo concepts, round 2 feedback" tells you, and your client, what happened. Short is fine. Use a consistent task name (design, development, meeting) and a short description of the actual work.

Daily checklist
1. Start a timer, or note the time, when you switch projects.
2. Before you stop for the day, add anything you missed.
3. Check each entry has a project, a task and a one-line note.
4. Mark admin and sales as non-billable.
5. Once a week, compare hours to your estimate on each fixed-price project.

Turn billable time into an invoice

Tracked time only pays you if it ends up on an invoice. At the end of the month, or when a project ends, gather the unbilled billable entries, group them in a way the client can read (by day or by type of work), multiply by your rate and add them as lines. On a fixed-price project, make it clear these are extra charges on top of the agreed fee, not part of it. For the rest of the process, from payment terms to follow-ups, see how to invoice clients.
